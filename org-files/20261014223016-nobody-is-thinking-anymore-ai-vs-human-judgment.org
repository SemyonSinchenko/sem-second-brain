#+title: Nobody Is Thinking Anymore: AI vs. Human Judgment
#+ROAM_REFS: https://yusufaytas.com/nobody-is-thinking-anymore
#+filetags: :article:

* Summary
Source: [[https://yusufaytas.com/nobody-is-thinking-anymore][https://yusufaytas.com/nobody-is-thinking-anymore]]
The author observes that in AI-heavy work environments, specs, code, and strategy documents are increasingly drafted by [[id:96a58b04-1f58-47c9-993f-551994939252][LLM]]s, leaving very little genuine thinking underneath. The core argument is not anti-AI — the author uses AI daily — but a warning that we are treating LLMs like oracles rather than next-token predictors, outsourcing the very judgment we are supposed to develop and own. The article argues for keeping humans in the loop for decisions, embracing slow and friction-filled thinking, and preserving the routine work that builds judgment and taste, especially for newcomers who have never worked without these tools.

* Key Takeaways
- AI slop is replacing the judgment people are supposed to produce; LLMs are next-token predictors, not oracles.
- People and organizations that outsource decisions to AI remain accountable for them anyway.
- Imperfect, human-made trade-offs are valuable precisely because a person stands behind the writing and lives with the consequences.
- Skill atrophy is real: automation removes the "drills" that taught us how things work underneath, and understanding disappears with them.
- Not all routine work is waste — it is how judgment and taste are earned; taste will survive even as LLMs write the code.
- Real thinking is slow: reading, arguing, being challenged, getting it wrong, and returning to the problem produce deeper understanding than any shortcut.
- Summarizing is not understanding — a summary can be produced without anyone understanding anything.
- LLMs tell you that you are right every time; sometimes you need to be wrong to develop your own judgment.
- The critical line: letting AI help write code is fine; letting it decide *which* code is worth writing is not — that choice must remain human.

* Notes
** The core problem: judgment outsourced
- The author sees specs half-written by AI, code 80% written by AI, and strategy documents 60% written by AI across the industry (relevant to [[id:d8c9bdbb-4530-4863-bc8a-520d64105270][Agentic Coding]] culture).
- The danger is not AI usage itself but that AI output is being confused with what we should believe, do, or work on.
- A strategy document written by AI effectively tells an organization what it should be good at — as if we no longer trust ourselves to make that call.
- People in companies and even governments rely on LLMs for decisions, then discover they are still the ones accountable.

** What good human judgment looks like
- Sitting with the problem, taking a walk, listening to hard feedback, arguing over alternatives, getting a bit angry, and making imperfect trade-offs yourself.
- Imperfection is a feature: we don't know what's absolutely right anyway; what matters is that a person stands behind the writing.
- The author fears a "Wall-E" trajectory: if we don't draw the line ourselves, automation eventually decides what's good for us too.

** Skill atrophy and the value of routine
- Automation removes the manual steps (e.g., shell scripting by combining commands) that once taught us what was happening underneath.
- Losing the drills loses the understanding — so not all routine work is waste; it builds the judgment you later need.
- Taste survives because it is earned through years of routine experience, even when code is written by LLMs.

** Slow thinking beats shortcuts
- The author experimented with vibe coding and AI-drafted documents but consistently got better answers by staying with the problem: reading, explaining, being challenged, noticing gaps, and retrying.
- Friction is valuable — like going to bed with a stubborn bug and letting the brain search every corner until the epiphany comes. There is no shortcut for that.
- Practical practice: the author now asks newcomers to write down what they understood instead of asking AI to summarize. Summarizing is not understanding; understanding is a years-long process of explaining, being challenged, and correcting.

** The line to draw
- Nobody is saying to stop using AI — the author uses it daily.
- But we must catch ourselves handing over things we should have sat with longer.
- Final distinction: AI helping write code vs. AI deciding which code is worth writing. If we hand over the latter, "nobody is thinking anymore."
